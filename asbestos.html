Asbestos: real risk, badly communicated

9 October 2026

The honest version

I survey buildings for asbestos for a living. The deaths are real. The UK's rate is the highest in the world. That does not mean every scrap of cement sheet in a cupboard is a ticking bomb.

Asbestos in good condition, left alone, releases very little fibre. That is why UK law says manage it rather than rip it out: the duty to manage under the Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012, regulation 4. Dose matters hugely. The amphiboles — blue crocidolite and brown amosite — are far more potent than white chrysotile. But the World Health Organization (WHO) said in 2014: “No threshold has been identified for the carcinogenic risk of asbestos, including chrysotile.” Disease appears 20–50 years after exposure, which is why people dying now were mostly exposed decades ago.

The media rarely distinguishes fibre type, dose or condition. Half the world still mines and buys the stuff and gets little coverage. Both of those facts can be true at once.

The UK numbers

According to the Health and Safety Executive (HSE), there were 2,146 mesothelioma deaths in Great Britain in 2024 (2,255 in 2023; average 2,508 a year over 2011–2020). That is about 32 per million against a GB population of 67.3 million mid-2024 (ONS).

Most people dying now were exposed in the 1960s–80s in shipyards, construction and lagging. The peak of use is long past; the peak of deaths has been slow to follow because of that long latency.

How the UK, Germany, Spain and the EU regulate it

United Kingdom

  • Blue and brown banned 1985; white banned November 1999.
  • Licensing since 1983; duty to manage non-domestic buildings since 2004.
  • Control limit 0.1 f/cm³ over 4 hours.
  • Not bound by the new EU limit post-Brexit, so still at 0.1.

Germany

  • Total ban 1993 (Gefahrstoffverordnung).
  • Buildings started after 31 October 1993 presumed asbestos-free.
  • Works notified a week ahead.
  • Asbest-Richtlinie ranks weakly bound asbestos in buildings by urgency: immediate repair / reassess at least every 2 years / at least every 5.

Spain

  • Ban by Order of 7 December 2001, in force 14 June 2002; installed products allowed to end of life.
  • No owner duty to manage nationally. Ley 7/2022 required councils to compile a census of sites with asbestos by about April 2023, with highest-risk public sites managed before 2028.
  • Every removal job needs a work plan approved by the labour authority.
  • Limit still 0.1 f/cm³ over 8 hours; Spain missed the December 2025 deadline for the EU's new 0.01 limit.

European Union

  • Directive 1999/77/EC banned chrysotile with deadline 1 January 2005.
  • Directive (EU) 2023/2668 cuts the limit tenfold to 0.01 f/cm³ from late 2025 (electron microscopy by 2029).

World use is down about 55% since 2000. Brazil banned asbestos in 2017, yet a mine in Minaçu kept exporting under a Goiás state law until Brazil's Supreme Court struck that law down on 18 September 2026 with immediate effect (whether the mine has actually stopped is not yet confirmed).

Biggest importers in 2023: India about 485,000 t, China about 196,000 t, then Uzbekistan, Thailand, Sri Lanka, Tajikistan and Vietnam.

India

No mining (new leases stopped 1986, renewals stopped 1993, by government decision). Workplace limit 0.1 f/cm³ since 2013. White asbestos is imported freely for cement roofs and pipes. The Supreme Court refused a ban in 2011. Kerala has been replacing school asbestos roofs since 2019.

Russia

White asbestos is legal under a 1998 decree saying controlled use “guarantees” safety; amphiboles are banned. Workplace limits are set by dust mass (mg/m³) not fibre count — a far looser yardstick. The town of Asbest sits beside the world's largest active open-pit chrysotile mine (Uralasbest). There is no national mesothelioma register.

Trade and the treaty

The EU and UK ban imports (REACH Annex XVII entry 6) but exports are controlled by notification/consent rather than banned outright. Australia and Canada ban both import and export. The US EPA 2024 rule bans import with phase-outs to 2036.

Under the Rotterdam Convention, chrysotile has been blocked from its prior-informed-consent list at every meeting since 2006. At the 2025 meeting six parties objected (official summary). Campaign group IBAS names Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, India, Zimbabwe, Laos and Belarus. The next meeting is April 2027.

Worldwide

WHO/ILO 2016 estimate: about 209,000 deaths a year from occupational asbestos exposure, mostly lung cancer, about 23,000 mesothelioma. GLOBOCAN 2022: about 25,400 mesothelioma deaths worldwide.

My take

What actually protects people on site is knowing where it is — registers, surveys — not headlines. The people most at risk now are electricians, plumbers, joiners and DIYers who disturb it unknowingly. Panic removal can do more harm than leaving intact material alone. The risk is real; the scaremongering about every undisturbed board is not the same thing as honesty about dose, fibre type and who is still digging the stuff up.
